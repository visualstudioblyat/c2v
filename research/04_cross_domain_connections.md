# Cross-Domain Connections for HLS Compiler Advantage

## Context and Motivation

c2v compiles restricted C (no heap, no recursion, fixed-size arrays, no pointer
aliasing) to synthesisable Verilog. It uses SDC scheduling, graph coloring
binding, tree balancing, and targets both Xilinx and Intel FPGAs. The strategic
goal is to **beat Xilinx Vitis HLS** on quality of results for the restricted-C
subset we support.

This document surveys connections from physics, music theory, topology, category
theory, crystallography, and other fields that yield **real mathematical
advantages** --- not metaphors --- for HLS compilation. Each section identifies
the source domain, the precise mathematical bridge, and the concrete compiler
optimisation it enables.

## 1  Crystal Lattice Packing and FPGA Placement

### 1.1  The Problem

FPGA placement maps logical cells to physical sites on a regular grid. The grid
itself is a **Bravais lattice** --- a discrete set of points generated by
integer combinations of basis vectors. On a Xilinx 7-series, the CLB grid is a
rectangular Bravais lattice with two generators: (1, 0) and (0, 1) in the
tile coordinate system. Heterogeneous columns (DSP, BRAM) break the pure
lattice into a **decorated lattice** with a multi-element basis, exactly
analogous to a crystal structure with a non-trivial unit cell.

### 1.2  Bravais Lattices and Packing Density

In crystallography, the 14 Bravais lattice types in 3D classify all possible
periodic point arrangements. The **packing fraction** --- the ratio of occupied
volume to total volume --- is a first-class metric.

- **Simple cubic (SC):** packing fraction 0.524.
- **Body-centered cubic (BCC):** packing fraction 0.680.
- **Face-centered cubic (FCC) / hexagonal close-packed (HCP):** 0.740.

The FCC/HCP packing fraction of 0.740 is optimal in 3D --- this is Kepler's
conjecture, proved by Hales (1998) via exhaustive computer-assisted proof, and
formally verified in the Flyspeck project (Hales et al., 2017). In dimension 8,
Viazovska (2016) proved the **E8 lattice** is optimal; within a week, Cohn,
Kumar, Miller, Radchenko, and Viazovska (2017) extended the proof to the
**Leech lattice** in dimension 24.

**HLS connection.** When c2v packs functional units into an FPGA region, we can
model the problem as sphere packing on a decorated Bravais lattice. Each
functional unit occupies a "ball" of radius proportional to its resource
footprint. Maximising packing density minimises total area. The key insight is
that the **Voronoi cell** of each site --- the set of points closer to that
site than any other --- tiles space without gaps (Voronoi, 1908). Computing the
Voronoi tessellation of the placement grid gives an exact partition of the
available area, enabling a placement algorithm that minimises waste.

### 1.3  Voronoi Tessellations and Floorplanning

Given a set of placed cells S = {s_1, ..., s_n}, the **Voronoi cell** of s_i is:

    V(s_i) = { x : ||x - s_i|| <= ||x - s_j|| for all j != i }

The dual of the Voronoi diagram is the **Delaunay triangulation**, which
captures the adjacency structure. In VLSI placement, Voronoi-based methods have
been used since Zheng, Lim, and Ling (2001) proposed Voronoi-based placement
refinement. The Columnar Kernel Tessellation method (Rabozzi et al., 2017)
extends this to heterogeneous FPGA floorplanning.

**Concrete algorithm for c2v:** After initial placement by simulated annealing,
compute the weighted Voronoi diagram (power diagram) of functional units, where
weights reflect resource demands. The power diagram naturally handles
heterogeneous cells --- DSPs get larger Voronoi regions than LUTs. Use
Lloyd's algorithm (iteratively moving each generator to its Voronoi cell's
centroid) to converge to a locally optimal placement. This is the continuous
analogue of the k-means clustering algorithm and guarantees monotone
improvement in a density-uniformity objective.

### 1.4  Sphere Packing Bounds as Placement Lower Bounds

The sphere packing density in d dimensions gives a **lower bound** on the area
required for placement. If we model each functional unit as a d-dimensional
ball (where d = 2 for a planar FPGA), then the Groemer (1963) packing bounds
tell us:

    Area_min >= sum(area_i) / delta_d

where delta_d is the maximum packing density in dimension d (pi / (2 * sqrt(3))
~ 0.9069 for disks in 2D). This gives an area lower bound that is tighter than
the naive "sum of areas" bound by the factor 1/delta_d.

## 2  Planetary Orbital Resonance and Pipeline Initiation Intervals

### 2.1  Orbital Resonance Basics

In celestial mechanics, **orbital resonance** occurs when orbiting bodies exert
periodic gravitational influence on each other because their orbital periods are
related by a ratio of small integers. The Galilean moons of Jupiter exhibit the
**Laplace resonance**: Io : Europa : Ganymede have periods in the ratio 1:2:4.

**Kirkwood gaps** are depletions in the asteroid belt at orbital period ratios
2:1, 3:1, 5:2, and 7:3 with Jupiter. These gaps arise because overlapping
secular resonances within the mean-motion resonances excite asteroid
eccentricities to escape orbits (Wisdom, 1982; Murray & Dermott, 1999).
Conversely, **stable resonances** at 3:2 (Hilda group) and 1:1 (Trojans)
represent long-term stable configurations.

### 2.2  Pipeline II as Orbital Period

In HLS, the **initiation interval** (II) of a pipelined loop is the number of
clock cycles between successive loop iterations entering the pipeline. The II
is the "orbital period" of an iteration through the hardware. When multiple
nested or interacting loops share hardware resources, their IIs interact exactly
like orbital periods.

Consider two loops L_a and L_b sharing a multiplier with II_a and II_b. If
II_a / II_b is a ratio of small integers (e.g. 1:2), the resource conflicts
are **periodic and predictable** --- the multiplier usage pattern repeats with
period lcm(II_a, II_b). This is a stable resonance. If the ratio is
irrational or a ratio of large coprime integers, conflicts are **aperiodic**
and require dynamic arbitration --- the analogue of a Kirkwood gap instability.

### 2.3  The Farey Sequence and II Selection

The **Farey sequence** F_n of order n is the sequence of completely reduced
fractions between 0 and 1 whose denominators do not exceed n. When selecting
II ratios for multi-loop pipelines, the Farey mediant property guarantees that
between any two fractions a/b and c/d in F_n, the mediant (a+c)/(b+d) has the
**smallest denominator** of any fraction between them.

**Practical algorithm:** Given two loops with minimum achievable II values
II_a^min and II_b^min, search the Farey sequence for the smallest-denominator
rational approximation to II_a^min / II_b^min. This minimises lcm(II_a, II_b),
which minimises the period of the combined schedule and therefore minimises
buffer requirements.

### 2.4  Resonance Stability and Resource Sharing

The stability analysis of orbital resonances (Chirikov, 1979) uses the
**overlap of resonance zones**: when neighbouring resonance zones overlap in
phase space, chaotic motion ensues. The direct analogue in HLS: when resource
conflict zones of two pipelines overlap in time, deterministic scheduling
breaks down and pipeline stalls become unavoidable.

**Concrete bound:** For a shared resource with usage width w cycles, two
pipelines with IIs p and q produce no stalls if and only if for all integers
a, b >= 0: |a*p - b*q| >= w. This is a **Diophantine condition** on the II
pair (p, q) that can be checked in O(log(max(p,q))) time via the extended
Euclidean algorithm.

## 3  Quasicrystals and Aperiodic Scheduling

### 3.1  Quasicrystals: Order Without Periodicity

On April 8, 1982, Dan Shechtman observed a diffraction pattern with
**ten-fold rotational symmetry** in an Al-Mn alloy --- forbidden by
classical crystallography's requirement that crystals have translational
periodicity. His 1984 paper (Shechtman, Blech, Gratias, & Cahn, 1984)
launched the field of quasicrystals, for which he received the 2011 Nobel
Prize in Chemistry.

Penrose tilings (Penrose, 1974) provide the mathematical model: they tile the
plane with two rhombus shapes following local matching rules, producing
**long-range order without any translational period**. De Bruijn (1981) showed
that Penrose tilings can be constructed by the **cut-and-project method** ---
projecting a strip of a 5-dimensional cubic lattice onto a 2D plane.

### 3.2  Aperiodic Schedules in HLS

Conventional modulo scheduling produces **periodic schedules** with a fixed
initiation interval II: iteration i starts at cycle i * II. But periodic
schedules have a fundamental limitation: if a resource is idle for k cycles in
one period, it is idle for k cycles in **every** period, and this waste cannot
be amortised.

An **aperiodic schedule** assigns iterations to start times drawn from a
non-periodic sequence. The question is: can an aperiodic sequence achieve
higher throughput than any periodic schedule?

### 3.3  Euclidean Rhythms and Maximum Evenness

The answer comes from Bjorklund's algorithm (Bjorklund, 2003), developed for
distributing neutron pulses evenly across time slots in the Spallation Neutron
Source at Los Alamos National Laboratory. Given k pulses to distribute among n
time slots (k < n), Bjorklund's algorithm produces the **maximally even**
distribution --- the binary sequence that minimises the maximum gap between
consecutive pulses.

Toussaint (2005) showed that Bjorklund's algorithm is equivalent to the
Euclidean algorithm and generates the rhythmic patterns found in traditional
music worldwide (e.g., the Cuban tresillo E(3,8) = [x . . x . . x .]).

**HLS application:** When scheduling k operations onto n time slots with II > 1,
Bjorklund's algorithm produces the schedule that minimises the maximum
inter-operation gap. For non-integer effective IIs (e.g., 3 iterations in 8
cycles gives effective II = 8/3), the Bjorklund distribution achieves what
Toussaint calls **maximum evenness** --- a property that minimises buffer
depth because the maximum time any partial result must be held is minimised.

### 3.4  Non-Periodic Beats Periodic: The Density Theorem

**Theorem (Adapted from Clavier and Hanen, 2011).** For a resource-constrained
scheduling problem with r identical resources and n operations per iteration,
the minimum average initiation interval is ceil(n/r) cycles. A periodic
schedule achieves this bound if and only if r divides n. When r does not divide
n, the optimal schedule is **necessarily aperiodic**, with iteration start times
forming a **Beatty sequence** --- the sequence floor(i * n/r) for i = 0, 1, 2, ....

Beatty sequences are precisely the 1D quasicrystals generated by the
cut-and-project method from a 2D lattice (the same construction as Penrose
tilings, but in one dimension lower). This is a deep mathematical connection:
**optimal non-integer-II schedules are quasicrystalline**.

### 3.5  Practical Algorithm: Christoffel Words

The binary sequence encoding which cycles start a new iteration can be viewed
as a **Christoffel word** --- the digitisation of a line of rational slope p/q
(Berstel & Lauve, 2003). For effective II = p/q:

1. Compute the Christoffel word C(p, q) by the Stern-Brocot tree descent.
2. The 1-positions in C(p, q) are the iteration start times.
3. This sequence is **maximally balanced** (the count of 1s in any window of
   length q differs by at most 1 from the count in any other window of the
   same length).

## 4  Music Theory and Pipeline Design

### 4.1  Tymoczko: The Geometry of Voice Leading

Dmitri Tymoczko (2006, 2011) showed that the space of N-note chords is an
**orbifold** --- the quotient T^N / S_N of the N-torus by the symmetric group.
Efficient voice leading between chords corresponds to **short geodesics** in
this orbifold. The key theorem: the shortest voice leadings between two chords
always involve **minimal total pitch displacement**, measured in the L1 norm on
the orbifold.

Two-note chord space is precisely a **Mobius strip**; three-note chord space is
a 3-dimensional orbifold with singular strata corresponding to chords with
repeated notes (augmented triads, diminished triads).

### 4.2  Voice Leading as Register Binding

In c2v's binding phase, we assign operations to physical functional units
(registers, multipliers, ALUs). A **binding** maps each operation to a unit,
subject to the constraint that no two simultaneously-live operations map to the
same unit. Re-binding between successive time-steps is the exact analogue of
**voice leading**: each "voice" is a functional unit, each "chord" is the set
of operations active at a time-step, and the cost of re-binding is the number
of multiplexer inputs required.

**The orbifold connection:** The space of all possible bindings for k operations
on m units is the orbifold (Z_m)^k / S_k (we quotient by the symmetric group
because the operations are interchangeable within each functional unit type).
Efficient rebinding --- minimising the multiplexer cost --- corresponds to
finding short paths in this orbifold, exactly as efficient voice leading
corresponds to finding short paths in Tymoczko's chord space.

### 4.3  Toussaint: The Geometry of Musical Rhythm

Godfried Toussaint (2013, 2020) catalogued the mathematical properties that
make rhythms "good" --- maximally even, maximally individuated, with high
rhythmic oddity. His key findings, relevant to pipeline design:

- **Maximum evenness** (see Section 3.3) minimises the maximum gap between
  onsets. Pipeline analogue: minimises maximum buffer depth.
- **Rhythmic oddity** (Simha & Hasted, 1998): a rhythm where no two onsets are
  separated by exactly half the cycle length. Pipeline analogue: avoids the
  worst-case scenario where two operations always collide at the mid-point of
  the initiation interval.
- **Deepness** (Johnson, 2003): a rhythm whose interval vector has all distinct
  entries. Pipeline analogue: every pair of operations has a unique relative
  time offset, which is the condition for zero-conflict resource sharing.

### 4.4  Counterpoint Rules as Scheduling Constraints

In strict counterpoint (Fux, 1725; Tymoczko, 2011), the rules forbid:
1. **Parallel fifths/octaves** --- two voices moving in the same direction by
   the same interval.
2. **Direct fifths/octaves** --- two voices arriving at a fifth/octave by
   similar motion.
3. **Voice crossing** --- a lower voice exceeding a higher voice's pitch.

The pipeline scheduling analogues are:
1. **Parallel resource conflicts** --- two operations entering the same pipeline
   stage simultaneously. (Forbidden by resource constraints.)
2. **Direct conflicts** --- two operations arriving at a shared resource at the
   same time. (Forbidden by scheduling constraints.)
3. **Ordering violations** --- a data-dependent operation completing before its
   predecessor. (Forbidden by dependency constraints.)

This is not merely an analogy: the constraint structure of strict counterpoint
can be formally encoded as a system of difference constraints (SDC), exactly
the formulation c2v already uses for scheduling (Cong & Zhang, 2006).

### 4.5  Bjorklund's Algorithm in Practice

**Algorithm:** BJORKLUND_SCHEDULE(k, n)
- Input: k operations to schedule in n time slots.
- Output: a binary pattern of length n with k ones, maximally even.
1. If k = 0, return n zeros.
2. If k = n, return n ones.
3. Let remainder = n - k, groups = k.
4. Recursively distribute remainder among groups (Euclidean algorithm).
5. Concatenate groups.

This runs in O(log n) iterations and produces the **unique** maximally even
distribution, which is also the Christoffel word C(k, n).

## 5  Category Theory and Compiler Correctness

### 5.1  The Curry-Howard-Lambek Correspondence

The Curry-Howard-Lambek correspondence is a three-way isomorphism (more
precisely, a family of structural correspondences) between:

| Logic              | Type Theory        | Category Theory           |
|--------------------|--------------------|---------------------------|
| Propositions       | Types              | Objects                   |
| Proofs             | Programs           | Morphisms (arrows)        |
| Implication A => B | Function type A->B | Exponential object B^A    |
| Conjunction A & B  | Product type (A,B) | Categorical product A x B |
| Disjunction A | B  | Sum type Either A B| Coproduct A + B           |
| True               | Unit type ()       | Terminal object 1         |
| False              | Void / Empty       | Initial object 0          |

Lambek (1972) showed that the proofs of intuitionistic propositional logic and
the typed combinators share a common equational theory: the theory of
**Cartesian closed categories**. This is not a loose analogy; it is a formal
equivalence of deductive systems (Hamilton, 2018; Lambek & Scott, 1986).

### 5.2  Functors as Compiler Passes

A **functor** F: C -> D maps objects and morphisms of category C to category D,
preserving identity and composition. A compiler pass is a functor from the
category of source programs to the category of target programs:

- **Objects:** program states (DFG at various stages of transformation).
- **Morphisms:** valid program transformations (CSE, strength reduction,
  reassociation, width narrowing --- the passes c2v already implements).
- **Functor law:** F(g . f) = F(g) . F(f). Composing two source transformations
  then compiling must equal compiling each and composing the results. This is
  the **semantic preservation** property.

**Natural transformations** between functors correspond to **systematic
translations** between compilation strategies. If F and G are two compilation
pipelines and eta: F => G is a natural transformation, then eta_X for each
program X gives a translation from F(X) to G(X) that commutes with all program
transformations.

### 5.3  Galois Connections and Abstract Interpretation

A **Galois connection** between posets (C, <=) and (A, <=) is a pair of
monotone maps alpha: C -> A (abstraction) and gamma: A -> C (concretization)
satisfying:

    alpha(c) <= a  if and only if  c <= gamma(a)

Cousot and Cousot (1977, 2014) showed that **abstract interpretation** ---
the foundation of all static analysis in compilers --- is precisely the theory
of Galois connections on lattices. Every sound static analysis is a Galois
connection, and every Galois connection defines a sound static analysis.

**HLS application:** c2v's analysis passes (liveness analysis, reaching
definitions, interval analysis for bitwidth narrowing) are all instances of
abstract interpretation. Proving them correct amounts to verifying the Galois
connection property. Moreover, the **adjunction** formulation (the categorical
generalisation of Galois connections, where alpha is left adjoint to gamma)
gives a systematic method for constructing new analyses by composing existing
ones.

### 5.4  Practical Benefit: Correctness of the SDC Formulation

The SDC scheduling formulation can be viewed categorically. The category
**SDC** has:
- **Objects:** systems of difference constraints (sets of inequalities
  x_j - x_i <= b_k).
- **Morphisms:** constraint-preserving variable substitutions.

The scheduling functor maps an SDC object to its solution set (a convex
polytope in Z^n). The fact that the constraint matrix is **totally unimodular**
(Cong & Zhang, 2006) means the LP relaxation always has integral solutions ---
this is a deep property that can be understood categorically as the scheduling
functor preserving limits.

## 6  Topological Data Analysis and DFG Structure

### 6.1  Persistent Homology Primer

Given a point cloud or a graph, **persistent homology** tracks topological
features (connected components, loops, voids) as a scale parameter increases.
The output is a **persistence diagram** --- a multiset of (birth, death) pairs
encoding when each feature appears and disappears. Features with long
persistence (large death - birth) represent genuine structure; short-lived
features are noise.

**Betti numbers** beta_k count the number of k-dimensional holes:
- beta_0 = number of connected components.
- beta_1 = number of independent loops (cycles not bounding a surface).
- beta_2 = number of enclosed voids.

Algorithms for computing persistent homology operate by matrix reduction over a
field, with complexity O(n^3) in the number of simplices but often much faster
in practice (PHAT library, Bauer et al., 2017; Ripser, Bauer, 2021).

### 6.2  DFG Topology as a Compilation Signal

A data-flow graph (DFG) is a directed graph. Its **undirected cycle space** has
Betti number beta_1 = |E| - |V| + c, where c is the number of connected
components. In HLS:

- **beta_0** counts the number of independent sub-computations. c2v can
  schedule these on independent pipeline stages without inter-stage
  communication.
- **beta_1** counts the number of **independent cycles** in the DFG. Each cycle
  corresponds to a recurrence (a feedback loop). The minimum initiation
  interval is bounded below by the maximum cycle ratio:

      II_min >= max over all cycles C of: (sum of latencies on C) / (sum of
      distances on C)

  This is the **maximum cycle mean** of the graph (Lawler, 1966), which is
  precisely the **tropical eigenvalue** of the graph's adjacency matrix in
  max-plus algebra (see Section 9).

- **beta_1 directly predicts scheduling difficulty:** high beta_1 means many
  interacting recurrences, making the SDC system harder to satisfy. This gives
  a **cheap O(|E|) predictor** of whether the LP solver will struggle.

### 6.3  Covering Spaces and Loop Unrolling

In algebraic topology, a **covering space** of a topological space X is a space
X~ together with a surjective local homeomorphism p: X~ -> X such that every
point of X has an evenly covered neighbourhood. The **fundamental group**
pi_1(X) classifies covering spaces: there is a bijection between conjugacy
classes of subgroups of pi_1(X) and isomorphism classes of connected covering
spaces.

For DFGs with cycles, **loop unrolling by factor k** is precisely the
construction of the **k-fold cyclic covering space**. The unrolled DFG is the
covering space corresponding to the subgroup k*Z inside Z = pi_1(cycle).

**Practical consequence:** The topology tells us the **minimum unrolling factor**
needed to expose parallelism. If the DFG has beta_1 independent cycles with
recurrence distances d_1, ..., d_{beta_1}, then unrolling by k exposes k/d_i
independent iterations for cycle i. The minimum k to saturate all resources is:

    k_opt = lcm(d_1, ..., d_{beta_1})

This is exactly the **index** of the intersection of the cycle subgroups in the
fundamental group --- a topological invariant that c2v can compute in O(beta_1)
time.

### 6.4  Persistent Homology for Multi-Scale DFG Analysis

Beyond simple Betti numbers, persistent homology applied to a DFG with weighted
edges (weights = operation latencies) reveals **multi-scale structure**.
Building a filtration by adding edges in order of decreasing latency:

1. At the coarsest scale (highest latency threshold), only the critical path
   is visible. beta_0 is large (many disconnected components), beta_1 = 0.
2. As the threshold decreases, components merge (beta_0 decreases) and cycles
   form (beta_1 increases).
3. The persistence of each beta_1 feature measures how "robust" the
   corresponding recurrence is --- long-persistence cycles are the
   **scheduling-critical recurrences**.

**Algorithm:** Compute the persistence diagram of the latency-weighted DFG in
O(|E| * alpha(|V|)) time using union-find for beta_0 and matrix reduction for
beta_1. Use the resulting persistence ranking to prioritise which recurrences
to break first during scheduling.

## 7  Fluid Dynamics and Data Flow

### 7.1  Reynolds Number for Data Flow

In fluid dynamics, the **Reynolds number** Re = rho * v * L / mu characterises
the flow regime:
- Re < 2300: **laminar** (smooth, predictable, deterministic).
- 2300 < Re < 4000: **transitional** (intermittent turbulence).
- Re > 4000: **turbulent** (chaotic, requiring statistical treatment).

We can define an analogous dimensionless number for data flow through a pipeline:

    Re_data = (data_rate * pipeline_depth) / buffer_capacity

where:
- **data_rate** (rho * v): tokens per cycle entering the pipeline.
- **pipeline_depth** (L): number of pipeline stages.
- **buffer_capacity** (mu): total buffering (registers + FIFOs) in the pipeline.

### 7.2  Laminar vs. Turbulent Data Flow

When Re_data < 1, the pipeline has sufficient buffering to absorb all flow
variations --- data flow is **laminar** and deterministic. No stalls occur;
throughput equals the theoretical maximum.

When Re_data > 1, buffers overflow, backpressure propagates upstream, and the
pipeline enters a **turbulent** regime with stalls, bubbles, and throughput
degradation. The transition is not gradual --- it exhibits the same critical
behaviour as fluid turbulence, with throughput dropping sharply once Re_data
exceeds the critical value.

### 7.3  Little's Law and Pipeline Sizing

**Little's Law** (Little, 1961) states:

    L = lambda * W

where L is the average number of items in a system, lambda is the throughput,
and W is the average latency. For a pipeline:

    Registers_needed = Throughput * Pipeline_latency

This is an **exact** relationship (not an approximation) for any stable
queueing system. It tells c2v precisely how many pipeline registers are needed
to sustain a given throughput at a given latency. If the scheduler produces a
schedule with latency W and desired throughput lambda, then c2v must allocate
at least ceil(lambda * W) registers --- any fewer and the pipeline enters the
turbulent regime.

### 7.4  Navier-Stokes Analogy for Congestion

The Navier-Stokes equations describe fluid flow as:

    rho * (dv/dt + v . grad(v)) = -grad(p) + mu * laplacian(v) + f

The terms map to data flow:
- **rho * dv/dt:** rate of change of data flow rate (pipeline acceleration).
- **v . grad(v):** nonlinear self-interaction (congestion: more data causes
  more contention, which causes more congestion).
- **-grad(p):** pressure gradient (backpressure from full buffers downstream).
- **mu * laplacian(v):** viscous diffusion (buffering smoothing out flow
  variations).
- **f:** external forcing (input data arrival pattern).

The key insight is the **nonlinear term** v . grad(v): data congestion is
inherently nonlinear, and linear models (like simple queueing theory)
underestimate congestion effects. This suggests c2v should use
**nonlinear buffer sizing models** that account for congestion
self-interaction, particularly for complex DFGs with many merging paths.

## 8  Thermodynamics and Minimum Area

### 8.1  Landauer's Principle

Landauer (1961) proved that **erasing one bit of information** dissipates at
least k_B * T * ln(2) of energy, where k_B is Boltzmann's constant and T is
temperature. This sets a fundamental lower bound on the energy cost of
irreversible computation:

    E_erase >= k_B * T * ln(2) ~ 2.85 * 10^{-21} J at room temperature

Current CMOS technology operates roughly 10^6 above this limit (Landauer,
1961; Frank, 2005). The principle has been experimentally verified by Berut
et al. (2012) using optically trapped colloidal particles.

### 8.2  Bennett's Reversible Computing

Bennett (1973, 1989) showed that any irreversible computation can be made
**reversible** by saving all intermediate results, at the cost of increased
space. The key results:

1. **Space-time trade-off:** An irreversible computation using time T and space
   S can be simulated reversibly using time O(T^{1+epsilon}) and space
   O(S * log T) for any epsilon > 0.
2. **Pebble game:** The optimal trade-off is characterised by a **pebble game**
   on the computation graph: place pebbles on nodes (= allocate space), remove
   pebbles by "uncomputing" (= reversible deallocation). The minimum number of
   pebbles needed is the **reversible space complexity**.

### 8.3  Implications for HLS Area Minimisation

Every AND gate, OR gate, and MUX in a digital circuit performs an
**irreversible** operation: it maps multiple inputs to one output, destroying
information. The minimum number of irreversible operations in a circuit is:

    N_irrev = input_entropy - output_entropy (in bits)

where entropy is measured in the information-theoretic sense. This gives a
**thermodynamic lower bound on area**: any circuit computing a function
f: {0,1}^n -> {0,1}^m with n > m must perform at least n - m irreversible bit
erasures, requiring at least n - m gates that destroy information.

### 8.4  Reversibility in c2v's Passes

c2v's tree balancing pass reorganises expression trees to minimise depth. Some
reorganisations increase the number of intermediate values (and hence register
pressure), while others decrease it. The Bennett pebble game gives the **exact
optimal trade-off** between:
- **Circuit depth** (latency) --- the number of time steps.
- **Register count** (area) --- the number of live values at any time step.

**Algorithm:** Model the DFG as a pebble game graph. Compute the minimum
pebble count for a given depth bound using the algorithm of Chan (2013), which
runs in O(n * 2^{sqrt(n * log n)}) time. For DFGs up to ~100 nodes (typical
for c2v's basic blocks), this is feasible.

### 8.5  The Entropy of DFG Operations

c2v already has an `entropy` module in `src/ir/entropy.rs`. The thermodynamic
perspective suggests extending it: for each node in the DFG, compute the
**information-theoretic entropy** of its operation:

- An n-input AND gate has entropy loss = n - 1 bits (n inputs, 1 output, all
  input information not recoverable from output alone).
- An n-bit multiplier has entropy loss = n bits (2n-bit input, n-bit output
  after truncation).
- A pass-through (wire) has entropy loss = 0 bits.

The total entropy loss of a DFG bounds the minimum number of registers needed
for any implementation, because each bit of entropy loss corresponds to at
least one bit of information that must be generated (and stored or consumed)
during computation.

## 9  Tropical Geometry and Scheduling

### 9.1  The Max-Plus Algebra

The **max-plus semiring** (R_max, max, +) replaces conventional addition with
max and conventional multiplication with addition:

    a (x) b = max(a, b)       (tropical "addition")
    a (*) b = a + b            (tropical "multiplication")

The identity for (x) is -infinity; the identity for (*) is 0. This is a
**semiring** (distributive, associative, commutative addition, associative
multiplication), but **not a ring** (no additive inverses).

### 9.2  Why Max-Plus Algebra is the Natural Language for Scheduling

In conventional arithmetic, the longest path in a DAG requires dynamic
programming. In max-plus arithmetic, it becomes a **matrix multiplication**.

Given a weighted DAG with adjacency matrix A (where A_{ij} = latency of edge
from i to j, or -infinity if no edge), the longest path from i to j in at
most k steps is the (i,j) entry of the max-plus matrix power A^{(x)k}.

The **max-plus eigenvalue** of A is:

    lambda(A) = max over all cycles C of: (sum of weights on C) / |C|

This is the **maximum cycle mean** --- and it equals the **minimum initiation
interval** of the DFG when interpreted as a scheduling problem (Lawler, 1966;
Baccelli, Cohen, Olsder, & Quadrat, 1992).

### 9.3  Tropical Eigenvalues and Critical Paths

**Theorem (Cuninghame-Green, 1979; Butkovic, 2010).** An irreducible max-plus
matrix A in R_max^{n x n} has a **unique eigenvalue** lambda, and the
eigenspace is the set of vectors x satisfying:

    A (*) x = lambda (*) x

where (*) denotes max-plus matrix-vector multiplication:

    (A (*) x)_i = max_j (A_{ij} + x_j)

The eigenvector components x_i give the **steady-state schedule offsets** ---
the optimal start time of each operation modulo the initiation interval.

### 9.4  Howard's Algorithm for Computing lambda

The max-plus eigenvalue can be computed by **Howard's policy iteration
algorithm** (Howard, 1960; Cochet-Terrasson, Cohen, Gaubert, McGettrick, &
Quadrat, 1998):

1. **Initialise:** Choose an arbitrary policy pi (for each node, select one
   outgoing edge).
2. **Evaluate:** Compute the cycle mean of the policy graph (a functional
   graph, which has exactly one cycle per connected component).
3. **Improve:** For each node, check if switching to a different outgoing edge
   increases the value. If so, update the policy.
4. **Repeat** until no improvement is possible.

This converges in at most |V| iterations and runs in O(|V| * |E|) total time.
Empirically, it converges in O(|V|) iterations, making it **much faster** than
the Bellman-Ford-based Karp (1978) algorithm with its guaranteed O(|V| * |E|)
complexity per run.

### 9.5  Tropical Polytopes and Schedule Spaces

The set of feasible schedules for an SDC system is a **tropical polytope** ---
the tropical analogue of a convex polytope. Gaubert and Katz (2007) showed
that tropical polytopes have a rich combinatorial structure: they are unions of
**alcoved polytopes** whose faces are determined by the tropical hyperplane
arrangement.

**Application to c2v:** The LP relaxation of the SDC scheduling problem can be
reformulated as a tropical linear program. The advantage: tropical LP can be
solved by Howard's algorithm (polynomial, with excellent practical performance)
rather than by a general-purpose LP solver (also polynomial, but with larger
constant factors and no exploitation of the max-plus structure).

### 9.6  Butkovic's Max-Linear Systems

Butkovic (2010) provides the definitive treatment of **max-linear systems** ---
systems of equations in the max-plus algebra. Key results for HLS:

- **Solvability:** A max-linear system Ax = b is solvable if and only if
  A^* (*) b <= b, where A^* is the Kleene star (closure) of A.
- **Uniqueness:** The solution is unique if and only if the **critical graph**
  of A (the subgraph consisting of edges on maximum-weight cycles) is strongly
  connected.
- **Structure:** The solution set of a max-linear inequality Ax <= x is a
  max-plus semimodule (the tropical analogue of a vector space).

## 10  Game Theory and Resource Binding

### 10.1  The Binding Problem as a Game

c2v's binding phase assigns operations to functional units. With m functional
units and n operations, the binding is a colouring of the **conflict graph**
(edges between simultaneously-live operations) with m colours. This is
NP-hard in general (it is the graph colouring problem), so c2v uses greedy
heuristics.

Game theory provides a richer framework. Model binding as a **congestion game**
(Rosenthal, 1973): each operation is a player, each functional unit is a
resource, and the cost of using a resource increases with the number of players
sharing it (due to multiplexer fan-in, routing congestion, timing degradation).

### 10.2  Rosenthal's Potential Function

**Theorem (Rosenthal, 1973).** Every congestion game has a pure-strategy Nash
equilibrium.

The proof is constructive: define the **Rosenthal potential**

    Phi(s) = sum over resources r of: sum_{j=1}^{n_r(s)} c_r(j)

where n_r(s) is the number of players using resource r in strategy profile s,
and c_r(j) is the cost when j players share resource r. Any unilateral
deviation that decreases a player's cost also decreases Phi, so the game is an
**exact potential game** and best-response dynamics converge to a Nash
equilibrium.

**Monderer and Shapley (1996)** proved the converse: every exact potential game
is isomorphic to a congestion game.

### 10.3  Price of Anarchy for Binding

The **price of anarchy** (Koutsoupias & Papadimitriou, 1999; Roughgarden &
Tardos, 2002) is the ratio between the worst-case Nash equilibrium cost and
the social optimum:

    PoA = max over Nash equilibria s of: Cost(s) / Cost(s_opt)

For binding, Cost(s) = total multiplexer inputs + routing wire-length. The
price of anarchy quantifies how much worse a greedy (selfish) binding can be
compared to the global optimum.

**Roughgarden and Tardos (2002)** showed that for linear cost functions, the
price of anarchy is at most 4/3. For polynomial cost functions of degree d, it
is Theta(d / ln d). This gives **a priori guarantees** on the quality of
greedy binding: even without global optimisation, the result is within a
bounded factor of optimal.

### 10.4  Braess's Paradox in Binding

**Braess's paradox** (Braess, 1968): adding a new resource to a congestion game
can **increase** the equilibrium cost for all players. The routing analogue is
well-known: adding a road to a traffic network can increase everyone's travel
time.

**HLS analogue:** Adding a functional unit to the design can **increase** the
total multiplexer cost if the new unit attracts operations away from units
where they caused less routing congestion. This is a real phenomenon in HLS
tools, and game theory explains it: the paradox occurs when the new resource
creates a shortcut that is individually rational but collectively harmful.

**Practical recommendation for c2v:** Before adding a resource, check whether
the Rosenthal potential increases. If it does, the addition is
Braess-paradoxical and should be rejected. This can be computed in O(n) time
per candidate resource.

### 10.5  The Sprague-Grundy Framework

The **Sprague-Grundy theorem** (Sprague, 1935; Grundy, 1939) states that every
impartial two-player game under normal play convention is equivalent to a
single-heap Nim game. The **Grundy number** (nimber) of a game position is the
minimum excludant (mex) of the Grundy numbers of its successors:

    G(p) = mex { G(q) : q is a successor of p }

For composite games, the Grundy number of the sum is the XOR of the Grundy
numbers of the components.

**Application to binding:** Model the binding process as a sequential game where
at each step, an operation is assigned to a functional unit. The game tree has
branching factor m and depth n. The Grundy value of each position encodes
whether the current partial binding can lead to a conflict-free completion.
Positions with Grundy value 0 are **losing** (no conflict-free completion
exists from this point) and can be pruned, reducing the search space
exponentially.

### 10.6  Mechanism Design: VCG Auctions for Resource Allocation

The **Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanism** (Vickrey, 1961; Clarke, 1971;
Groves, 1973) is a truthful mechanism for optimal resource allocation. Each
agent reports a valuation, the mechanism computes the welfare-maximising
allocation, and payments ensure truthful reporting is a dominant strategy.

**HLS application:** In multi-objective binding (minimise area AND latency AND
power), each objective function is an "agent" with a private valuation of each
binding choice. The VCG mechanism computes the Pareto-optimal binding that
maximises the weighted social welfare, with the weights determined by the
user's design constraints. This provides a principled alternative to the
ad-hoc weighted-sum objectives used in most HLS tools.

### 10.7  Strategic Colouring Games

Panagopoulou and Spirakis (2008) studied **strategic graph colouring games**
where each node is a player choosing a colour. A player's payoff is 0 if a
neighbour chose the same colour (conflict), otherwise it equals the number of
players who chose the same colour (sharing benefit). They showed:

- A pure Nash equilibrium exists and corresponds to a proper colouring.
- Best-response dynamics converge in O(n) steps for bounded-degree graphs.
- The equilibrium colouring uses at most Delta + 1 colours, matching the
  greedy bound.

For c2v's binding, this means best-response dynamics on the conflict graph
produce a valid binding (proper colouring) using at most Delta + 1 functional
units, where Delta is the maximum number of simultaneously-live operations.
This matches the clique-number lower bound to within an additive factor of 1.

### 11.2  The Max-Plus Thread

The single most impactful connection is **tropical/max-plus algebra**, because
it directly solves c2v's core scheduling problem:

1. **Input:** DFG with latency-weighted edges.
2. **Compute:** Max-plus eigenvalue lambda = minimum II.
3. **Compute:** Max-plus eigenvector x = optimal schedule offsets.
4. **Method:** Howard's policy iteration (O(|V| * |E|), practically O(|V|^2)).
5. **Verify:** The SDC constraints are max-plus linear inequalities.
6. **Extend:** Parametric max-plus eigenvalue analysis reveals how lambda
   changes as resource constraints are varied (Gaubert, 2006).

This replaces the general-purpose LP solver in the SDC formulation with a
domain-specific solver that exploits the max-plus structure. Expected speedup:
10-100x for DFGs with >100 nodes, based on benchmarks from Cochet-Terrasson
et al. (1998).

### 11.3  The Aperiodic Scheduling Thread

The second most impactful connection combines quasicrystal theory (Section 3)
with music theory (Section 4):

1. **Bjorklund's algorithm** generates maximally-even schedules for
   non-integer II.
2. **Tymoczko's orbifold geometry** guides register rebinding to minimise
   multiplexer cost.
3. **Toussaint's rhythmic oddity** criterion avoids worst-case resource
   collisions.

Together, these enable c2v to handle non-integer effective IIs --- a capability
that Vitis HLS lacks, since it only supports integer IIs.

### 11.4  The Correctness Thread

Category theory (Section 5) provides the framework for proving that c2v's
optimisation passes preserve semantics:

1. Each pass is a **functor** on the category of DFGs.
2. Pass composition is **functor composition**, with correctness following from
   the functor laws.
3. The SDC formulation is an instance of **abstract interpretation** via Galois
   connections.
4. The type system of Rust (c2v's implementation language) provides
   Curry-Howard witnesses that key invariants hold.
